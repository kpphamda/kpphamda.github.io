Pho (Vietnam)

Rice noodle soup in a slow-simmered beef or chicken broth, topped with herbs and lime.

Pho appeared in northern Vietnam in the early 1900s, around Hanoi and Nam Dinh. Beef was not widely eaten before the French colonial period, and cooks began simmering the leftover bones into a spiced broth. After the country was divided in 1954, northerners moving south brought pho with them, and the southern version grew sweeter and came piled with herbs and bean sprouts.

Main crop: rice

Mapo Tofu (China)

Soft tofu in a spicy Sichuan sauce with chili, fermented bean paste, and minced meat.

Mapo tofu comes from Chengdu in Sichuan and dates to the late 1800s. The name refers to the pockmarked woman who is said to have run the restaurant where it was first sold to passing workers. Its heat comes from two directions at once: chili paste for spice and Sichuan peppercorn for the numbing tingle called ma la.

Main crop: soybeans

Sushi (Japan)

Vinegared rice served with raw fish, vegetables, or egg, often wrapped in seaweed.

Sushi began as a preservation method, not a quick meal. Fish was packed in fermenting rice for months, and the rice was thrown away. In Edo period Tokyo, vendors sped this up by seasoning rice with vinegar and serving it with fresh fish, creating the hand-pressed nigiri sold from street stalls in the 1820s.

Main crops: rice, soybeans

Kimchi (South Korea)

Fermented cabbage seasoned with chili, garlic, ginger, and fish sauce.

Koreans have fermented vegetables in brine for well over a thousand years, but the red kimchi people picture today is newer: chili peppers only reached Korea in the 1600s. Families traditionally prepare a winter supply together each late autumn, a custom called kimjang that UNESCO added to its intangible cultural heritage list in 2013.

Main crop: cabbage
